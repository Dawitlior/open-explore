You made your first three trades of the day cleanly — patient entries, correct sizing, stops respected. By the fifth, something has changed. The setup is looser, the size is off, the stop feels optional. You are the same trader with the same strategy, but you are no longer the same *decision-maker*. This is **decision fatigue**: the measurable degradation of judgment as the number of decisions you make accumulates. It is physiological, it is predictable, and it is quietly responsible for a huge share of avoidable losses.

---

## 1. Willpower Is a Depleting Resource

Every deliberate decision draws on the same finite pool of cognitive control, governed largely by the prefrontal cortex. Each choice — enter or wait, size up or down, hold or cut — spends a little of that budget. As the budget depletes, the brain does not stop deciding; it starts **cutting corners.** It defaults to the low-effort option: impulsive entries, ignored rules, "close enough" setups.

The famous illustration comes from outside trading: studies of parole boards found that the probability of a favourable ruling started high in the morning, decayed steadily to near zero before breaks, and reset sharply after the judges ate and rested. The judges hadn't changed their principles — their *capacity to apply* them had eroded with each successive case. Your trading day is the same courtroom.

---

## 2. The Decay Curve of Judgment

We can model the quality of your $n$-th decision of the day as a declining function of accumulated cognitive load:

$$ Q(n) = Q_0 \cdot e^{-\kappa n} + Q_{\min} $$

where $Q_0$ is your fresh, peak judgment, $\kappa$ is your personal fatigue rate, and $Q_{\min}$ is the floor your decisions collapse toward when depleted. The implication is direct: the *expected quality* of each additional trade falls, which means the **expected value** of each additional trade falls with it.

At some decision count $n^{*}$, the expected edge of taking another trade drops below zero — not because the market changed, but because *you* did. Every trade past $n^{*}$ is, on average, a donation.

---

## 3. The Data In Your Own Journal

This is not folklore — it is measurable in your log. Tag every trade with its **sequence number within the day** (1st, 2nd, 3rd…) and compute expectancy by sequence:

*   Bucket trades by their intraday order.
*   Compute win rate and average R for each bucket.
*   Plot expectancy against trade number.

The overwhelmingly common finding is a clear downward slope: the first two or three trades carry the day's positive expectancy, while trades four, five, and beyond bleed it back. Your own data will name your personal $n^{*}$ — the trade number past which you should simply stop.

---

## 4. Engineering Around a Depleting Brain

You cannot willpower your way out of a willpower shortage. You engineer around it:

*   **Hard daily trade caps.** If your data says expectancy dies after the third trade, make three a rule enforced *before* the session, when your judgment is still fresh. The best decision of the day is the one that removes future decisions.
*   **Pre-committed checklists.** Convert discretionary judgment into a fixed procedure. A checklist doesn't fatigue; it lets a tired brain execute a fresh brain's standards.
*   **Reduce trivial decisions.** The pool is shared across *all* choices, not just trading ones. This is why disciplined operators standardize everything peripheral — same routine, same environment — to reserve cognitive budget for the market.
*   **Protect the peak.** Trade your highest-conviction setups early, when $Q(n)$ is near $Q_0$. Don't spend your best judgment on marginal opportunities and arrive at the A+ setup depleted.
*   **Scheduled resets.** Breaks, food, and stepping away genuinely restore capacity — the parole-board reset is real. A ten-minute walk can be worth more than any indicator.

---

## 5. The Counter-Intuitive Truth

More screen time and more trades feel like more effort, more diligence, more chances to win. The mathematics says the opposite: past your fatigue threshold, additional activity has *negative* expectancy. The disciplined trader's edge is often not a better setup but a **smaller number of decisions made at higher quality.** Doing less, on purpose, is a performance strategy.

---

## Conclusion

Your judgment is a battery, not a constant. It starts the day charged and drains with every decision, and the market is happy to collect on your depleted choices. Measure expectancy by trade sequence, find the point where your edge turns negative, and cap your activity *before* you reach it. The fifth trade isn't usually a bad setup — it's a tired trader. Protect the brain, and the discipline protects the account.

Trade fewer decisions. Make each one when you can still afford it.
